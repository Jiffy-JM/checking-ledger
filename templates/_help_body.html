Getting started

The ledger answers one question: what will your checking account look like in two weeks, a month, or a year? You give it a starting balance and the money you expect to come in and go out, and it projects the balance day by day.

  1. Enter your current balance and, if you like, your paycheck.
  2. On Transactions, add what repeats: rent, bills, subscriptions, transfers.
  3. If your paychecks vary, add your hourly pay there too: your rate, usual hours, and payday.
  4. Add each credit card once, with the day its statement closes and the day payment is due.
  5. Check Overview. The chart shows where the balance is heading and the lowest point on the way.

Drag across the chart, or move your mouse over it, to read the balance on any day. The buttons above it switch between 2 weeks and 1 year.

How balances work

A balance is what your account held at the start of its date, before anything dated that day. Anything dated the same day counts on top of it.

Update your balance on Overview whenever you check your bank. Type what the bank shows, or leave the box blank to accept the ledger's own figure. Each update becomes the new starting point for every projection.

After an update, History shows how far the bank's figure was from what the ledger expected. A gap usually means spending that isn't in the ledger yet.

"Lowest point" is the smallest balance between today and the end of the range you picked. If it goes below zero, Overview shows a warning with the date.

Transactions

Recurring transactions repeat weekly, every 2 weeks, monthly, or yearly from their first date. A monthly item on the 31st lands on the last day of shorter months.

One-time transactions happen once. Past ones move to History when you update your balance, because that balance already includes them.

Tap any transaction to change it. Pause keeps it in the ledger but leaves it out of every projection. Delete removes it for good.

Each transaction has a category. Overview adds them up under "Next 12 months by category" so you can see where the money goes each month.

Hourly pay

Use this when your paychecks change from one payday to the next. On Transactions, add your hourly rate, the hours in a usual week, how often you are paid, and any one payday.

That gives your base pay: usual hours times your rate for one pay period, before taxes. For example, 40 hours a week at $25.00, paid every 2 weeks, is 80 hours and $2,000.00.

Each time you are paid, open the pay and enter your take-home, the amount that reached your account. If you worked more or fewer hours than usual, enter the hours too.

From your last six paychecks the ledger works out what share of your earnings you keep after taxes and deductions. Every future payday is then estimated as base pay times that share. With hours entered, a week of overtime raises that paycheck without inflating every later estimate.

Before you have entered a paycheck, the estimate is base pay times the starting take-home percent you gave, or all of it if you left that blank.

Overview reminds you when a payday has arrived and its paycheck isn't entered. Until you enter it, the estimate stands in for it.

If your pay never changes, a recurring transaction is simpler. Use one or the other for the same job, not both.

Credit cards

Add each card once on Transactions with two days of the month: the day the statement closes and the day payment is due. From then on, every future due date has a payment in your projection.

The amount of each payment comes from one of two places:

  • A statement you entered. When a statement closes, Overview reminds you. Open the card, pick the due date, and type the statement balance.
  • An estimate, until you do. Choose "Recent statements" to use the average of the card's last six statements, or "A set amount" to use a figure you type.

Estimates are marked "estimate" in the Overview list and on the chart. The list covers the same stretch as the chart, so choose 3M or longer to see payments further out. Entering a statement replaces the estimate for that month only.

If a due date passes and you never entered the statement, the estimate keeps standing in for it. When you next update your balance, estimates dated before it are saved as they stood and marked "estimate" in History, so past figures don't shift later. They are left out when working out future averages.

A new card has no statements to average, so give it a set amount to start with. The ledger assumes you pay each statement in full on its due date.

Pausing a card stops its estimates. Deleting a card keeps the statements you entered as ordinary one-time transactions.

What if

On Overview, What if lets you try a one-off amount before committing to it: a repair, a trip, a bonus. Enter the amount and date and choose Try it.

The chart redraws with it included. A dashed line shows your projection without it, and the panel tells you how the lowest point changes.

Nothing is saved while you are trying. Keep it opens the add-transaction form already filled in, so you can name it and save. Clear discards it.

History and card spending

Balance updates lists every balance you recorded, the change since the one before, and the gap from what the ledger expected. "Undo latest update" removes the most recent one.

Credit card spending adds up credit card payments between balance updates. If you update your balance on the first day of each quarter, each row becomes one quarter.

Settled one-time transactions are the one-time items dated before your latest balance.

Net worth

Add each account once: savings, retirement, brokerage, credit cards, loans. Mark it as an asset or a debt.

Whenever you check your accounts, type the balances under Record balances. Leave an account blank to keep its last figure. Enter debts as the amount you owe.

Each date you record becomes a snapshot on the chart. Net worth is assets minus debts. It is tracked separately from the checking projection.

Your data files

Everything is kept in two plain CSV files in the data folder on your server: ledger.csv and networth.csv. There is no database.

To edit in bulk, use Download CSV on History or Net worth, change the file in Numbers or Excel, export it as CSV, and upload it on the same page. The upload replaces everything. The current file is copied to data/backups first, and the newest 20 copies are kept.

If any row has a problem, nothing changes and the page lists each row and what to fix. Dates like 10/6/2026 and amounts like $1,450.00 or (25.00) are accepted.

In ledger.csv, each row has a type: balance, recurring, onetime, or card. Negative amounts are money out. A card row uses description for its name, close_day and due_day for its two days, estimate (average or fixed), and amount for its set amount. A statement is a one-time row whose card column holds that card's id. An hourly pay row has type pay, with hours, rate, frequency, a payday in date, and an optional percent; a paycheck is a one-time row whose pay column holds its id.

Cloud backup

An optional second container can send encrypted copies of your data to cloud storage, such as Cloudflare R2, every night. It is off until you set it up. This app itself never connects to the internet and never sees the storage keys.

Your data is encrypted on your server, with a password only you hold, before anything is uploaded. Keep that password somewhere off the server. Without it nobody can open the backups, including you.

To turn it on, copy backup.env.example to backup.env, fill in the storage address, keys, and password, and run docker compose --profile backup up -d. The README has the full steps for R2.

History shows when the last backup ran. Overview shows a warning if a backup fails or none has finished for two days.

Run these from the folder the app was set up in. None of them can change your live data.

  • docker compose logs backup shows each backup, whether it worked, and when the next one is due.
  • docker compose --profile backup run --rm backup once makes one extra backup right now.
  • docker compose --profile backup run --rm backup snapshots lists every backup in storage with its ID and date.
  • docker compose --profile backup run --rm backup check verifies the stored backups are complete and undamaged.
  • docker compose --profile backup run --rm backup restore downloads the latest backup into the restore folder. Add an ID from the snapshots list to fetch an older one.
  • docker compose --profile backup up -d --force-recreate backup restarts the backup service after you change backup.env.

A restore never overwrites your live data. Once you have checked the files, stop the app, copy restore/data into data, and start the app again. The README lists those commands one by one.

Updating the app

Your data lives in the data folder, outside the app's container. Updating replaces the container and leaves that folder alone.

Take a copy of data first, then either fetch newer files with git pull and rebuild with docker compose up --build -d, or set LEDGER_IMAGE in .env to a published version and run docker compose pull ledger followed by docker compose up -d --no-build.

If you use cloud backup, also run docker compose --profile backup up -d --force-recreate backup. It replaces only the backup container so it uses the newer backup script, deletes nothing, and makes one backup straight away.

Always run these from the same folder. Started somewhere else, the app finds an empty data folder and looks like a fresh install; your real data is still where it was.

Themes

Theme at the top of every page changes the colors and whether the app is light, dark, or follows your device. Each browser remembers its own choice, so your phone and computer can differ.

Money in is always green and money out is always red, whichever theme you pick. The theme's accent colors the buttons, links, and chart.

To change what new browsers start with, set THEME and THEME_MODE in .env on your server.

Privacy and security

The app makes no connections to the internet. Fonts, styles, and scripts are served from your own server, and your browser is told to load nothing from anywhere else.

Set APP_PASSWORD in .env. Without it, anything that can reach the app on your network can read your ledger. Lock signs out the browser you are using; changing the password signs out every browser.

Traffic on your home network is not encrypted, so don't open the app's port to the internet through your router.

On an iPhone, use Share and then Add to Home Screen to open the ledger like an app.
